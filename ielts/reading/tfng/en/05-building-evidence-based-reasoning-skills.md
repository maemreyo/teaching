# Part V: Building Evidence-Based Reasoning Skills: A Systematic Process

The analysis of linguistic distractors and cognitive traps has shown that approaching the T/F/NG question type with mere tips or isolated tactics is insufficient. Common advice like "scan for keywords" or "pay attention to modifiers" ⁵ is necessary but only as individual tools. To effectively deal with such a complexly designed challenge, candidates need a systematic process, a structured reasoning framework that helps manage cognitive load, counter biases, and navigate ambiguity methodically.

## 5.1. From Tactics to Process

A systematic process shifts the candidate's approach from passive reaction to active analysis. Instead of just looking for an answer, the candidate learns to build a "case" for each option, based entirely on the evidence presented in the text. This process integrates effective tactics into a logical workflow, based on principles of evidence-based reading strategies that have been researched, such as self-questioning, clarifying, and monitoring comprehension.⁴¹ The goal is to turn the internal, often unconscious and error-prone, thought process of an expert into a set of external, clear, and learnable steps.

## 5.2. The 5-Step Evidence-Based Reasoning (E-BR) Framework

The E-BR process is designed to slow down the thinking process, forcing the candidate to consider each element deliberately, thereby minimizing errors caused by haste and cognitive biases.

### Step 1: Statement Deconstruction

This is the foundational step, aimed at deeply understanding the statement before engaging with the text. Instead of skimming, the candidate must perform a linguistic "autopsy."

- **Key Action:** Read the statement carefully. Identify and separate the core proposition – the main idea the statement wants to assert. Then, identify and circle all potential "semantic distractors" classified in Part II (quantifiers, comparatives, adverbs, verbs of certainty, etc.).
- **Cognitive Purpose:** This conscious labeling action activates the brain's attention, preparing it to specifically check these elements in the following steps. It turns potential traps into clear verification targets.

### Step 2: Hypothesis Formulation

This is a crucial meta-cognitive step, performed before searching the text. It directly counters the tendency to jump to conclusions.

- **Key Action:** Based on the deconstruction in Step 1, the candidate must explicitly state the necessary and sufficient conditions for each answer.
  - "For this statement to be True, the text must clearly state that..."
  - "For this statement to be False, the text must say the opposite, for example..."
  - "If the text talks about [the general topic] but does not mention [the specific detail/comparison/quantity], then the answer will be Not Given."
- **Cognitive Purpose:** This step creates an objective logical framework for evaluating evidence. It shapes the upcoming search and provides a clear standard for comparison, instead of relying on a vague feeling of "seems right" or "seems wrong."

### Step 3: Evidence Search

This step applies the skill of scanning with a clear purpose.

- **Key Action:** Use the keywords from the core proposition (identified in Step 1) to scan the text and locate the relevant sentence(s). A key advantage of the T/F/NG question type is that the answers usually appear in order in the passage, which helps to narrow down the search area.⁴
- **Cognitive Purpose:** The goal of this step is not to find the answer immediately, but to accurately identify the "crime scene" - the part of the text that contains the potential evidence.

### Step 4: Evidence Mapping

This is the heart of the process, where the deepest analysis takes place.

- **Key Action:** Place the statement and the evidence sentence(s) from the text side-by-side (either mentally or in quick notes). Perform a component-by-component comparison. Map the core proposition, and most importantly, map each "distractor" identified in Step 1 to the information in the text.
- **Legalistic Questions to Ask:**
  - Does the quantifier in the question ("all") match the word in the text ("many")?
  - Does the text perform the exact comparison that the question asks for?
  - Does the text support the entire meaning of the statement, or just a part of it?.⁴
  - Is the level of certainty expressed in the text ("suggests") equivalent to the level in the question ("proves")?
- **Cognitive Purpose:** This step forces a detailed, precise comparison, preventing the overlooking of subtle details. It shifts the task from "reading comprehension" to "evidence mapping."

### Step 5: Conclusion Validation

This is the final step to make a decision and perform a final meta-cognitive check.

- **Key Action:** Based on the mapping result in Step 4, make the final decision: True, False, or Not Given. Then, ask yourself explicitly:
  - "Am I assuming any connection?"
  - "Am I making a gap-filling inference that is not supported by the text?"
  - "Am I overlooking a small but important word (e.g., 'not', 'only') because I want the answer to be True/False?"
- **Cognitive Purpose:** This step is a direct defense mechanism against the cognitive biases discussed in Part IV, especially confirmation bias. It forces the candidate to play the role of their own "devil's advocate" before finalizing the answer.

By consistently following the E-BR process, candidates can transform a complex and error-prone cognitive task into a manageable sequence of operations. This process does not guarantee immediate speed, but it builds accuracy and confidence, and through practice, it will become faster and more automatic.

**Table 5.1: The E-BR (Evidence-Based Reasoning) Framework**

| Step | Step Name                    | Key Action                                                                                                     | Guiding Meta-cognitive Question                                                                                    |
| ---- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1    | **Statement Deconstruction** | Read the statement carefully, identify the core proposition and all potential semantic distractors.            | "What is this statement really asserting? Which words (quantifiers, comparatives, etc.) could change its meaning?" |
| 2    | **Hypothesis Formulation**   | Clearly state the necessary conditions for the answer to be True, False, or Not Given before reading the text. | "What does the text need to say for this to be True? To be False? When would it be Not Given?"                     |
| 3    | **Evidence Search**          | Use keywords from the core proposition to scan the text and locate the relevant sentence(s) or paragraph.      | "Where is the information about this topic in the passage?"                                                        |
| 4    | **Evidence Mapping**         | Compare each component of the statement (especially the distractors) with the evidence in the text.            | "Is every part of the statement confirmed or contradicted by the text? What is the exact logical relationship?"    |
| 5    | **Conclusion Validation**    | Make a final decision and double-check for biases or unsupported inferences.                                   | "Am I 100% sure based on the text, or am I inferring? Did I miss any small words?"                                 |
