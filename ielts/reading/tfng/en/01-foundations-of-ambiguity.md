# Part I: The Foundations of Ambiguity: Definition and Cognitive Classification

The True/False/Not Given (T/F/NG) question type in the IELTS Reading test has long been recognized as one of the biggest challenges for candidates, often seen as a source of ambiguity and unnecessary point loss. However, this ambiguity is not a flaw in the test's design, but a deliberately calibrated feature aimed at assessing a set of higher-order cognitive and linguistic skills. To decode the nature of this question type, we must first deeply analyze the official definitions, position it within the system of IELTS question types, and clearly distinguish it from the similar Yes/No/Not Given format. This analysis reveals that the difficulty of T/F/NG lies not in general text comprehension, but in the application of a strict binary logic process within a three-option decision space, where the distinction between "contradictory evidence" and "absence of evidence" becomes paramount.

## 1.1. Analysis of the Official Definitions

The foundation for understanding any question type lies in a thorough analysis of the definitions provided by the test-making body itself. According to official IELTS materials, the three options are defined as follows¹:

- **True:** The statement agrees with the information in the reading passage. This means the meaning of the statement must completely match the information provided in the text. This match is not necessarily a literal repetition; in fact, it is often expressed through paraphrasing, using synonyms or different sentence structures to convey the same meaning. Candidates must be able to recognize the semantic equivalence between two different expressions.
- **False:** The statement contradicts the information in the reading passage. This is a crucial and often misunderstood definition. "Contradiction" means the passage must present directly opposing information or make the statement logically impossible. For example, if the statement says "Coffee arrived in Europe after the 17th century," but the passage states it arrived "in the latter half of the 17th century," there is a clear temporal contradiction.⁴ The answer is 'False' not simply because the information is incorrect, but because there is positive evidence in the text to prove it wrong.
- **Not Given:** There is no information on this in the passage, or the information provided is insufficient to determine if the statement is True or False. This third option creates the complexity in the logical system of the question type. It requires the candidate to recognize the absence of decisive evidence. A part of the statement might appear in the text, but if its entire meaning is not confirmed or refuted, the answer must be 'Not Given'.⁴

The core distinction, and also the main source of ambiguity, lies between 'False' and 'Not Given'. 'False' requires the presence of contradictory evidence, while 'Not Given' is characterized by the absence of any evidence.⁵ The challenge for the candidate is to decide when the text's silence is equivalent to 'Not Given' and when a detail in the text actually creates a logical contradiction.

## 1.2. Positioning T/F/NG in the Cognitive Classification of IELTS Reading

Each IELTS Reading question type is designed to test a different set of cognitive skills. Positioning T/F/NG within this classification system helps clarify its specific cognitive demands. Researchers have analyzed IELTS question types based on two dimensions: "level of engagement" – whether the reader needs to interact with local details or the global meaning of the text; and "type of engagement" – whether the interaction is literal or interpretative/inferential.⁷

Based on this framework, T/F/NG is classified as a task that primarily requires **local-literal engagement**. This means this question type tests the candidate's ability to locate and verify specific, detailed information within a small portion of the text. It does not require the candidate to grasp the main idea of a whole paragraph (like in Matching Headings) or to infer the author's deeper implications. A study by Moore et al. (2012) showed that T/F/NG is one of the five core question types that require candidates to quickly find detailed information.⁸ This focus on detail explains why small words, modifiers, can completely change the answer. The candidate's task is not to understand the "big picture," but to forensically check whether a specific piece of information is confirmed, contradicted, or ignored in the text.

## 1.3. Distinguishing from Yes/No/Not Given

To further sharpen the cognitive domain of T/F/NG, distinguishing it from the Yes/No/Not Given (Y/N/NG) format is crucial. Although the three-option logical structure is similar, their assessment goals are fundamentally different.

- **True/False/Not Given** assesses **factual information** presented in the text.² Candidates are working with facts, events, or objective descriptions.
- **Yes/No/Not Given** assesses the **opinions, views, or claims** of the author or people mentioned in the text.² Candidates must determine if the statement aligns with the writer's attitude or argument.

This difference is very important as it guides the candidate's search process. With T/F/NG, they look for objective data. With Y/N/NG, they look for signs of subjective opinion, such as verbs of opinion (e.g., believes, argues, suggests) or evaluative adjectives.

Understanding the definitions and cognitive position of T/F/NG reveals an important conclusion. Its ambiguity is not accidental. The three-choice logical system, combined with the requirement to focus on local-literal details, deliberately creates a challenging decision space between "contradiction" and "lack of information." When a candidate doesn't find a direct match, they must face a higher-order question about the nature of the evidence: "Is the information I have sufficient to make a definitive judgment?" The test, therefore, not only assesses reading comprehension but also the ability to perform a micro-level epistemological judgment. This meta-cognitive requirement is the core of the designed ambiguity in the T/F/NG task. Therefore, effective strategies must focus on training this specific evaluative-logical skill, rather than just general reading comprehension.
