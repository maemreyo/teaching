# Part IV: Cognitive Analysis (Chapter 2): Reasoning Biases in an Assessment Environment

The high-pressure and ambiguous environment of the T/F/NG question type not only overloads working memory but also creates ideal conditions for systematic reasoning errors, known in cognitive psychology as cognitive biases, to emerge. These biases are mental shortcuts (heuristics) that the human brain uses to make quick decisions, but they can lead to serious errors when logical and objective analysis is required.²⁹ The T/F/NG question type, with its specific structure, transforms the test from a language assessment into a direct confrontation with the candidate's own inherent biases.

## 4.1. Introduction to Cognitive Biases

Cognitive biases are systematic patterns of deviation from norm or rationality in judgment.²⁹ They are not the result of a lack of intelligence or intentional cheating, but are byproducts of the brain's information processing structure, which is optimized for efficiency rather than absolute accuracy. In the context of standardized tests, although designers always strive to ensure objectivity, biases can still appear, related to cultural assumptions, background knowledge, or the cognitive processing style of the test-taker.³⁰

## 4.2. Confirmation Bias: The Main Enemy in T/F/NG

Among the many cognitive biases, confirmation bias is the one with the most profound and direct impact on T/F/NG performance.

- **Definition:** Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's preexisting beliefs or hypotheses.³⁴
  Simply put, we tend to see what we want to see.
- **Mechanism in T/F/NG:** The process of a candidate being affected by confirmation bias often unfolds as follows:
  1.  **Initial Hypothesis Formation:** After reading a statement, the candidate quickly forms an initial impression, an intuitive hypothesis (e.g., "This sounds right" or "This seems wrong"). This hypothesis may be based on recognizing a few familiar keywords or a general fit with the topic of the passage.
  2.  **Biased Search for Information:** Instead of conducting a neutral search for evidence, the candidate begins to scan the text with a subconscious goal: to find evidence that confirms their initial hypothesis.³⁷ If the hypothesis is "True," they will look for confirming words. If the hypothesis is "False," they will look for a contradiction.
  3.  **Biased Interpretation of Information:** When faced with ambiguous or incomplete information, the candidate tends to interpret it in a way that fits their hypothesis.³⁷ They may overlook important modifiers or semantic nuances to make the evidence "fit" what they are looking for.
  4.  **Consequence:** If the initial hypothesis happens to be correct, the candidate will quickly find the evidence and choose the right answer. However, disaster strikes when the initial hypothesis is wrong. For example, if a candidate believes a statement is 'False' but cannot find direct contradictory evidence, confirmation bias will prevent them from easily abandoning that hypothesis. Instead of concluding that the information is 'Not Given', they may get stuck, re-reading the same passage over and over, believing that the contradiction must be somewhere. This not only wastes precious time but also increases cognitive load, leading to more errors.

## 4.3. Related Biases

Besides confirmation bias, several other biases also contribute to the difficulty of the T/F/NG question type:

- **Hard-Easy Effect:** This is the tendency to overestimate one's ability to complete difficult tasks and underestimate one's ability on easy tasks.²⁹ A candidate might look at a seemingly simple statement, assume it's "easy," and then just look for a superficial keyword match in the text to conclude it's 'True' without carefully checking the complex semantic details hidden beneath.
- **Illusion of Validity:** The tendency to overestimate the accuracy of one's own judgments, especially when the information appears to be consistent or correlated.²⁹ If a statement seems "plausible" or fits the general tone of the passage, the candidate may feel it is "valid" and choose 'True', even if the specific detail is not mentioned at all.

The interaction between these biases creates a dangerous cognitive spiral. An initial hypothesis is formed, confirmation bias leads a biased search, and the illusion of validity reinforces a wrong conclusion.

Ultimately, the core struggle in the T/F/NG question type, especially between the 'False' and 'Not Given' options, is essentially a test of the ability to overcome confirmation bias. To choose 'False', one must find positive evidence of a contradiction. To choose 'Not Given', one must come to the conclusion of an absence of evidence for both True and False. The human brain, as a pattern-recognition machine, is better equipped to find a presence (a contradiction) than to recognize an absence (a gap in information). Confirmation bias amplifies this tendency, making us want to "find" a 'True' or 'False' answer. Therefore, choosing 'Not Given' is not just an act of reading comprehension, but a meta-cognitive act that requires intellectual humility—the ability to admit "I cannot know from this text." The ambiguity of the test, therefore, is a direct measure of the candidate's ability to perform this act, resisting the strong pull of their own cognitive biases.
