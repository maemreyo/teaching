# Part III: Cognitive Analysis (Chapter 1): Inference Gaps and Cognitive Load

After decoding the linguistic elements used to create ambiguity, the next step is to delve into the cognitive processes that occur in the reader's mind. Why are these linguistic elements so effective at causing errors? The answer lies in the complex interaction between the natural human inference process during reading and the artificial constraints imposed by the test. The T/F/NG question type is not merely a reading comprehension test; it is a cognitive stress test designed to exploit and challenge the limits of working memory and the ability to control automatic inference processes.

## 3.1. Theory of Inference in Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension is not a passive process of receiving information from a page. Instead, it is an active process of meaning construction, in which the reader constantly makes connections to fill in the gaps left by the text. These connections are called inferences.¹⁹ Reading comprehension research often distinguishes between two main types of inferences:

- **Text-connecting inferences:** Also known as bridging inferences, this type of inference is necessary to create local coherence between adjacent sentences or clauses. They rely primarily on linguistic cues present in the text. A typical example is resolving anaphoric references, such as understanding that "his" in the sentence "Omar gave Veronica his jacket" refers to Omar.²⁰ These inferences are fundamental to understanding the text at a basic level.
- **Gap-filling inferences:** Also known as knowledge-based or elaborative inferences, this type of inference requires the reader to draw on their background knowledge or personal experience to supplement information not explicitly stated in the text.²⁰ A classic example used in many studies is the sentence pair: "The campfire started to burn out of control. Tom grabbed a bucket of water.".²⁰ To understand the causal link here, the reader must activate their background knowledge that "water is used to extinguish fire" and infer that Tom grabbed the bucket of water with the intention of putting out the fire. This is an automatic and essential process for achieving deep understanding in everyday reading.

## 3.2. T/F/NG and the "Inference Trap"

The nature of the T/F/NG question type lies in its creation of an "inference trap" by explicitly penalizing gap-filling inferences that are not supported by the text. The core rule of the test, "do not use any information you already know about the subject" ¹, is essentially a direct instruction requiring candidates to suppress this natural cognitive process. The conflict between natural reading habits and the artificial rules of the test is clearly demonstrated in how the three options are handled:

- To answer **True**, the link between the statement and the text must be supported by explicit evidence or safe text-connecting inferences.
- To answer **False**, the contradiction must also be based on the text.
- The **Not Given** answer often appears precisely at the point where a normal reader would make a reasonable gap-filling inference. The previously analyzed example about the Amazon rainforest is a perfect illustration: the text says the Amazon has "thousands of plant species," and the question asks if it has the "highest number."⁵ Someone with background knowledge might assume this is true, and that is a reasonable gap-filling inference. However, because the text does not state this, the rule of the test forces the answer to be 'Not Given'. Therefore, the test is examining the candidate's ability to recognize the boundary between what the text says and what the text suggests but does not confirm.

## 3.3. Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) and its Impact on Performance

Why is suppressing inference so difficult, especially under exam conditions? Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) provides a powerful explanation. CLT posits that human working memory—the cognitive space where we consciously process information—is a limited resource.²² When the amount of information and the processing operations required exceed the capacity of working memory, performance declines. CLT distinguishes between three types of cognitive load:

- **Intrinsic Load:** The inherent difficulty of the task itself, dependent on the complexity and interaction between the elements of information.²²
- **Extraneous Load:** The load generated by the way information is presented or by non-essential elements of the task, which wastes cognitive resources.²²
- **Germane Load:** The "good" load, related to the process of deep processing and the construction of knowledge schemas in long-term memory.²²

Applying CLT to the T/F/NG question type, we can see a combination that causes overload:

- **High Intrinsic Load:** The task itself is complex. It requires the candidate to: (1) hold the statement in working memory; (2) scan the text for relevant information; (3) analyze paraphrased structures and synonyms; (4) identify semantic distractors; and (5) apply a complex three-option logical framework to make a final decision.
- **High Extraneous Load:** External factors add to the burden. The strict time pressure of the test²⁷, dense and unfamiliar academic texts², and the deliberate presence of semantic distractors are all sources of extraneous load. They consume precious working memory resources that could otherwise be devoted to comprehension and analysis.

When the total cognitive load (intrinsic + extraneous) exceeds the threshold of working memory, overload occurs.²² The consequence is that the candidate may miss important details (like the word "not" or "only"), fail to recognize a subtle contradiction, or default to a seemingly plausible gap-filling inference because they no longer have the cognitive "energy" to perform a rigorous evidence check.

In summary, the T/F/NG question type is not just a reading comprehension test. It is designed as a cognitive paradox: to succeed, you must read less like a proficient real-world reader (who freely uses background knowledge) and more like a forensic logician operating within a closed, artificial system of rules. Its difficulty arises from this forced shift in cognitive processing style, performed under immense time pressure and cognitive load. Effective training for this question type must therefore include explicit instructions on how to recognize and inhibit these automatic inference tendencies and adopt a necessary "legalistic" mindset.
