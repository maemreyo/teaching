# Video Script Plan: Mastering True/False/Not Given (5 Videos)

This detailed plan outlines a 5-video series designed to demystify and conquer the True/False/Not Given (T/F/NG) question type in IELTS Reading. Each video builds upon the previous one, creating a logical learning path from foundational concepts to practical application.

---

### **VIDEO 1: The Rules of T/F/NG: From Core Definitions to Cognitive Traps**

**Video Script Prompt**

**Target a specific use case:**
"Help learners grasp the core definitions and the correct mindset for tackling T/F/NG questions. This video will explain the fundamental 'rules of the game', especially the crucial distinction between False and Not Given, before introducing common linguistic and cognitive pitfalls."

**Focus on a specific source:**
"Utilize content from three files: `01-the-foundation-of-ambiguity.md`, `02-linguistic-analysis.md`, and `03-cognitive-analysis-inferential-gaps.md`."

**Describe the show structure:**
"Start with a direct question: 'Do you know the most critical difference between 'False' and 'Not Given'? This is the key to mastering this challenging IELTS Reading question type.'"

"Next, dive into the **'Golden Rules'** from the official definitions (file 01):
*   **True:** The statement agrees with the information in the reading passage.
*   **False:** The statement **contradicts** the information in the reading passage. This means the passage must provide directly opposing information.
*   **Not Given:** There is **no information** about the statement in the reading passage, or the information provided is insufficient to determine if it's True or False.
Emphasize: 'The core distinction: **False = There is evidence to prove it wrong**, while **Not Given = There is an absence of evidence**.'"

"Then, establish the correct mindset: **'Be a forensic investigator, not a casual reader'** (file 01). Explain that T/F/NG is a 'local-literal' task. Your job is not to understand the 'big picture', but to forensically check if a specific piece of information is 100% confirmed in the text. No assumptions, no outside knowledge."

"Transition logically: 'Now that we understand the rules, let's see how test designers use these very rules to 'trap' us.' Introduce the concepts of **Semantic Distractors** (file 02) and **The Inference Trap** (file 03)."

"Create a summary screen with two columns:
*   **Column 1: Core Rules of the Game**
    *   False = Contradiction
    *   Not Given = Absence of Evidence
    *   Think like an investigator
*   **Column 2: Types of Traps (To be explored)**
    *   Linguistic Traps (Vocabulary)
    *   Cognitive Traps (Psychology)"

"Conclude with a discussion question: 'After understanding the rules, do you think the most common mistake students make is confusing False with Not Given, or True with Not Given? Why?'"

### **VIDEO 2: Decoding 4 Deadly Linguistic Traps**

**Video Script Prompt**

**Target a specific use case:**
"Equip learners with the ability to identify and neutralize the 4 most common types of linguistic traps in T/F/NG, transforming them from invisible pitfalls into clear warning signs."

**Focus on a specific source:**
"Utilize content from the specialized files: `11-quantifiers-and-absolutes.md`, `09-comparatives-and-superlatives.md`, `10-adverbs-of-frequency-and-degree.md`, and `08-verbs-of-modality-and-certainty.md`."

**Describe the show structure:**
"Open: 'In the previous video, we learned about the existence of 'Semantic Distractors'. In this video, we'll dissect the 4 most common types. Think of them as 4 'enemies' you need to recognize.'"

"**Enemy #1: Quantifiers & Absolutes** (File 11)
*   'These are the easiest to spot: `all, every, only, always, never, none`. They leave no room for exceptions.'
*   'Classic trap: The question uses `All`, but the passage only states `Some` or `Many`. -> The answer is almost always **False**.'

"**Enemy #2: Comparatives & Superlatives** (File 09)
*   'Any statement containing `more...than, -er, the most, the best`.'
*   'Golden rule: The comparison **must explicitly exist in the passage**. If the passage describes two entities but doesn't compare them -> The answer is always **Not Given**.'

"**Enemy #3: Adverbs of Frequency & Degree** (File 10)
*   'These are more subtle: `often, sometimes, mainly, quickly`.'
*   'Classic trap: The question adds an adverb that the passage doesn't mention. -> **Not Given**.'

"**Enemy #4: Verbs of Modality & Certainty** (File 08)
*   'The trap lies in the 'escalation' of certainty.'
*   'Passage uses uncertain words: `suggests, claims, believes, may`. Question uses certain words: `proves, knows, it is a fact`. -> **False**.'

"Create a summary screen of the 4 Enemies and their 'Weaknesses':
*   **Quantifiers & Absolutes:** Weakness -> Look for an exception.
*   **Comparatives & Superlatives:** Weakness -> Check if the comparison exists.
*   **Adverbs:** Weakness -> Check if the specific detail is mentioned.
*   **Verbs of Certainty:** Weakness -> Compare the level of certainty."

"Conclude: 'Now you can 'name' your enemies. In the next video, we'll explore why our psychology makes us so susceptible to their tricks.'"

### **VIDEO 3: Cognitive Traps & Conquering Confirmation Bias**

**Video Script Prompt**

**Target a specific use case:**
"Help learners identify and combat the biggest 'hidden enemy' in T/F/NG: Confirmation Bias. This video explains why our brains tend to deceive themselves and how to stay vigilant."

**Focus on a specific source:**
"Utilize content from the file: `04-cognitive-analysis-reasoning-biases.md`."

**Describe the show structure:**
"Open: 'Have you ever been convinced a statement was 'False', searched endlessly for contradictory evidence, only to waste time and find the answer was 'Not Given'? You're not alone. This is due to a psychological effect called **Confirmation Bias**.'"

"Explain the concept: 'This is our brain's tendency to seek out evidence that supports what it already believes to be true, and to ignore or downplay anything that contradicts it. We see what we want to see.'"

"Describe the 3 steps this bias 'attacks' you when answering T/F/NG:
1.  **Forming a Hypothesis:** 'You glance at the question and get an initial gut feeling: 'This sounds true.'
2.  **Biased Search:** 'Instead of searching objectively, your brain scans the passage with an unconscious goal: 'Find where it says this is true.'
3.  **Biased Interpretation:** 'When you encounter ambiguous information, you tend to twist its meaning slightly to fit your hypothesis, overlooking crucial words like 'only' or 'sometimes'.'"

"Emphasize the consequence: 'Confirmation bias is the primary reason why choosing 'Not Given' is so incredibly difficult. Our brains hate ambiguity and always want a clear 'True' or 'False' answer. Accepting 'no information' goes against our natural instinct.'"

"Create a summary screen:
*   **The Enemy: Confirmation Bias**
    *   Leads you to search for proof, not for verification.
    *   Keeps you stuck on your initial judgment.
    *   The biggest barrier to choosing 'Not Given'.
*   **The Weapon Against It:**
    *   Always ask: 'Is there any evidence that *disproves* my initial thought?'
    *   Actively look for both supporting and contradictory evidence."

"Conclude with a thought-provoking question: 'Think back to a time you got a T/F/NG question wrong. Were you a 'victim' of confirmation bias?'"

### **VIDEO 4: Building Evidence-Based Reasoning Skills: The 5-Step Process**

**Video Script Prompt**

**Target a specific use case:**
"Provide learners with a systematic, step-by-step process to tackle every T/F/NG question. This video transforms the task from an intuitive, hit-or-miss endeavor into a scientific, logical method."

**Focus on a specific source:**
"Utilize content from the file: `05-building-evidence-based-reasoning-skills.md`."

**Describe the show structure:**
"Open by summarizing the problems: 'We've learned about linguistic traps and cognitive biases. So, how do we systematically overcome them? The answer is a 5-step reasoning process, called **E-BR (Evidence-Based Reasoning)**.'"

"Introduce E-BR as a 'checklist' that helps you slow down, think deliberately, and avoid errors due to haste or bias."

"Present each of the 5 steps in detail, with a clear title and action:
1.  **Step 1: Statement Deconstruction:** 'Don't rush to read the passage. First, 'dissect' the question. Find the core proposition and circle all potential 'trap words' like `all, only, more than...`'
2.  **Step 2: Hypothesis Formulation:** 'Ask yourself: What must the passage say for this to be True? What must it say to be False? If neither, it's Not Given.'
3.  **Step 3: Evidence Search:** 'Use keywords from the core proposition to scan the passage and locate the relevant sentence(s) or section.'
4.  **Step 4: Evidence Mapping:** 'This is the most crucial step. Place the question and the evidence from the passage side-by-side. Compare component by component. Does everything match 100%?'
5.  **Step 5: Conclusion Validation:** 'Before deciding, ask one last time: 'Am I making any assumptions? Am I overlooking a 'not' or 'only' because I want it to be True/False?' This is your final check against confirmation bias.'"

"Create a summary screen, visually displaying the 5 steps of the E-BR process."

"Conclude with practical advice: 'Initially, this process might feel slow. But be patient. Practicing E-BR builds accuracy, and speed will naturally follow. Better slow and accurate than fast and wrong.'"

*   **'Step 3: Search'**: 'Scan for `aim`, `profit`. Find: `The aim had been for the tunnel to be used by wheeled vehicles to transport cargo...`'

*   **'Step 5: Validate'**: 'The information clearly contradicts. No assumptions. The answer is **False**.'"

"Continue with a 'Not Given' example (e.g., question 3) to demonstrate how the process helps identify the absence of evidence."
